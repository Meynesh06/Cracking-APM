  How is a project estimated?

Project estimation involves predicting the time, resources and costs required to develop and
launch a new product or feature. A few key considerations while coming up with the estimate
are product requirements, complexity of the product, the team’s expertise, availability of
resources (people, equipment) and risks. Some of the estimation techniques are:

-

Top-down estimation: Starts with the overall project budget and allocates resources to different
tasks and phases.

-  Bottom-up estimation: Starts with estimation at task level and sums up the costs of all the tasks

to get an overall estimate.

-  Analogous estimation: Uses historical data from similar products to estimate costs of the

-

product.
Three-point estimation: Uses three estimates for each task- the optimistic, pessimistic, and most
likely estimates. The final estimate is calculated by averaging the three estimates.

Q6. Do you have any other tips / suggestions for aspiring PMs?

Ans: Go through exponent videos and discuss cases with peers. Make sure that you put yourself into the
habit of looking at all the stakeholders, finding the one with the most volume and digging deep into the

  How do you perform a make versus buy analysis? What are the factors considered for the same?
Performing a make versus buy analysis is a critical decision-making process for a product

manager when determining whether to develop a certain component or feature in-house or
purchase it from an external vendor or partner.

Following are the factors you should consider for make vs buy analysis :

1. Define the Problem: Clearly define the component, feature, or service in question. Identify its
scope, purpose, and required functionalities.

2. Cost Analysis: Compare the costs associated with both options:

 a. In-House:

       - Direct development costs: labor, salaries, overhead, tools, equipment.

       - Indirect costs: training, management, infrastructure.

20

       - Opportunity costs: resources diverted from other projects.

  b. External Vendor:

       - Licensing fees, subscription costs, or purchase price.

      - Integration and customization costs.

      - Maintenance and support costs.

3. Expertise and Resources: Assess the company's existing expertise and resources. Consider
whether your team has the skills and capacity to develop and maintain the component in-house.
If not, external options might be more efficient.

4. Time-to-Market: Evaluate the time required for both options. In-house development may take
longer due to learning curves, development time, and testing. Purchasing from an external
vendor could lead to a quicker time-to-market.

5. Quality and Control: Determine the desired level of quality and control. In-house
development provides greater control over design, implementation, and customization.
Purchasing may limit customization but might offer a more mature and reliable solution.

6. Strategic Alignment: Consider how the decision aligns with the company's long-term strategy,
goals, and core competencies. Making in-house might provide a competitive advantage, while
buying could free up resources for core activities.

7. Scalability: Evaluate the potential for scalability in both options. Will the chosen solution
accommodate future growth and changes in demand?

8. Risk Assessment: Assess the risks associated with each option, including technical challenges,
vendor reliability, data security, intellectual property concerns, and potential disruptions.

9. Total Cost of Ownership (TCO): Calculate the TCO for each option, which includes initial costs,
ongoing operational costs, maintenance, upgrades, and potential switching costs.

10. Vendor Relationships: If considering a vendor, evaluate their reputation, financial stability,
track record, and ability to provide ongoing support.

11. Flexibility and Customization: Determine the level of flexibility and customization required.
In-house development offers greater customization options, whereas purchased solutions might
have limitations.

12. Regulatory and Compliance Considerations: Check if the purchased solution complies with
relevant industry regulations and standards.

13. Long-Term Viability: Consider the longevity of the solution. Will the vendor be around for
the long term? Is the technology likely to become outdated quickly?

Remember that the make versus buy decision is not always binary. Hybrid approaches, such as
developing a core component in-house and supplementing it with external solutions, can also be
considered. The analysis process should be well-documented, involving input from cross-
functional teams, and aligned with the company's strategic goals.
