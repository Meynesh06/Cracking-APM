you might call it. The purpose of writing this book is to give you insights into acing the ‘Product

Management’ interview for companies that visit IIMB during the summer-placement internship

process.

Some of you might be wondering, ‘What does Product Management mean?’ That’s ok. Many of

us didn’t know either what Product Management (called PM from now on) meant and how to go

about preparing for the role. We learnt along the way, a lot from material available on the internet

and a lot from seniors in our time.

‘You mentioned material. If there are resources out there already, why do we need this book?’

need for some standardization of the knowledge that was being passed on. Thus, this book is an

attempt at standardizing some of the advice and knowledge that we received throughout our own

prep and convey it in a way that makes prepping for the PM role more structured.

IIMB Placement
Process

5

Welcome, dear reader, to the IIMB summer-internship placement process. This experience might

be unlike any other placement experiences you have experienced so far, not only in terms of

balancing preparation and academics together, but also socially, and (for some) mentally.

So in order for things to not get overwhelming, let’s take things a bit slow and begin right at the

top. This chapter is going to give an outline of the IIMB summer-internship placement process.

Note that this is a concise summary of the process and misses out on many details.

6

7

Resume Preparation starts well into the first term itself, with the PRs providing you with the proper

format and assigning resume mentors who help you out during the resume building process. After

making you do multiple iterations of resume-building, and once the points on your resume are

almost finalized, the PRs will ask for proof of each point on the resume.

With the final verified resume, you can then apply to internship companies via the online portal.

Each company that comes on campus has different pre-requisites that you might have to consider

before applying to those companies. Some might have a work-experience bar, while others might

have a background preference (for example, candidates who have completed CFA).

While the online application process goes on, several companies hold Pre-placement Talks (PPTs)

that you can attend and get a better idea of the company that is giving the talk and the roles that

it is offering.

During this phase of applying online and attending PPTs, some companies already start releasing

resume-based shortlists of preferred candidates. Most companies though (and most PM

companies) release their shortlists maybe a few days before the actual placement dates. As

shortlists come out, some companies might have extra rounds of tasks/activities (such as making a

wireframe for a mobile application) that shortlisted candidates will have to complete in order to

be eligible for future interview rounds.

Apart from all this prep, you will also have to specifically prepare for the GDs and actual

interviews. This is the most important part of the process because no company takes a candidate

solely based on their resume. All companies will test your communication and other skills on the

GD/Interview day itself, no matter how polished your resume is.

A bit about the roles

With so many companies coming to recruit and so many roles being offered, it helps in dividing

the roles into segments so that preparation doesn’t get overwhelming. So let’s divide the roles

into 6 major sectors: 1) Product Management 2) General Management 3) Sales/Marketing 4)

Consulting 5) Finance 6) Other roles such as analytics, operations etc. Most companies offer roles

that fit neatly into one of the categories above. For example, Microsoft offers PM & Marketing

roles. Amazon offers PM, Program Manager, and Operations roles.

By fitting the roles into neat categories, preparation for the roles becomes easier, because now

you don’t have to prepare for companies, you have to prepare for sectors. During the beginning

of prep-season, you will hear a lot of this type of conversation:

8

A: Which sector are you preparing for?

B: I’m thinking of preparing for Product Management

A: Not finance or consulting?

B: No. I might do some prep, but my primary focus is on preparing for the PM role

Starting preparation in an organized manner begins with identifying the sector you want to

prepare for and utilizing resources specific to that sector. About that time, you will notice that

prep groups also begin forming. These groups are composed of people with similar sector-

interests.

A piece of advice here is that, prepare hard only for one role. The rationale behind this advice is

that since preparation for any role takes a lot of time and effort, preparing for more than one role

will dilute your effort that you put into each role. For example, preparing for the PM and finance

roles together are very hard to do because they require different knowledge sets and

diametrically opposite skills.

What is Product
Management

10

With the big picture out of the way, let’s take a step down the ladder and begin focusing on

Product Management. For many of you, you might be wondering ‘What do the words ‘Product

Management’ mean?’ To this question, there is no well-defined answer. If you try to find the

definition online, you will notice that many sources say many things. So let’s not rely on online

definitions and let’s try to figure out what a PM is by the PM way.

The PM way? It means breaking down a given problem into little solvable components, signifying

what a PM ideally does on a day-to-day basis. Beginning with our problem, the words ‘Product

Management’ can be broken down into two words: 1) Product 2) Management.

What is a Product? A good or service that a company sells. What is management? What you and I

hope to do in a few years with a degree from IIMB in hand. Combining these two definitions, a

PM can be defined as being kind of a manager of a product. Some books even call it as being a

‘CEO’ of the product.

Now being a manager, you will be responsible for the product, which means you will be

responsible for:

1)  Designing the features of the product
2)  Designing and building a product prototype
3)  Testing the prototype
4)  Making the required changes to the product prototype based on user feedback and

usability

5)  Designing and developing the final product
6)  Maintaining the product

In summary, you will be managing the entire product life-cycle all the way from the design stage

to the maintenance stage.

Imagine thinking through something from the start to the finish. The entire process involves a lot

of decision-making and planning and answering hundreds of questions while making those

decisions, with some of them being along the lines of:

1)  What features should I incorporate in the product?
2)  How should I build the prototype?
3)  What metrics should I be observing while carrying out prototype usability tests?

Some questions will be open-ended, while others will be specific and close-ended. And with so

many questions to answer and decisions to make, you will have to have a highly-structured

11

mindset and step-by-step thinking process in order to come up with some logic to your decisions.

As anyone who has built a product previously knows, during the product-building exercise, you

will have to talk to customers to find out their pain points. Since you have to talk to customers,

you will need communication skills. During the talking phase, you will have to use your

understanding of the technology to try to solve the customer’s pain. Sometimes the customers

might not even know they need the product, so you might have to market it to them. Wow huh?

That’s not the end. Let’s add some more limitations to whatever we have so far. What about

budget constraints? And resource constraints? No company in the world will allow you to operate

on an unlimited budget or with infinite timelines. All the decisions that you have to make will have

to be made under constraints, either cost-related, time-related, or resource-related ones.

And that should wrap everything that the perfect PM does in their lives. Of course, none of us are

going to be perfect PMs anytime soon, and especially not during the internship. So let’s stop

thinking about the ideal scenario, and get back closer to reality, which leads me to the next

Some resume pointers

So you are ready to start preparing for the PM GDs and interviews? Hang on. Not so fast. There

are lots of hygiene factors that you will have to take care off before beginning to prepare for the

interviews. One of the most important hygiene factors is your 1-page resume.

“Oh. But the resume is only a 1-pager. Will it take long to make?”

Yes, the resume will take very long to prepare. That’s why the exercise starts early in the first term

and continues until the second term. Resume preparation takes time because you will have to

draft points in the right manner and then get all of them approved by a third-party.

The resume-building process starts with you being assigned resume mentors. Depending on

when you get hold of this book, you might or might not have received enough gyan about

resume-building from your mentors. So let me also take this opportunity to pass on some resume

preparation gyan. But before that, I want to add a disclaimer that the forthcoming tips are meant

to be guides and not laws of nature. Adopt them if they make sense and fit the current context

you are in.

Gyan 1: No one knows how to prepare the perfect resume. You might have already received a lot

of tricks and tips to make the perfect resume. Some of them will be correct. Some of them might

even be contradictory in nature:

Mentor A: Remove the buckets in your resume because they consume too much space. Add some

more text

Mentor B: Put buckets in your resume because they bring out achievements

The truth is, no one really knows what companies look for in resumes, and so they guide you

based on their personal experience, which might or might not be appropriate for you. Take all

advice and follow the tips that suit your judgement

Gyan 2: Don’t remove technical points from your resume. This is a common mistake that many

people make, and in the case of a PM resume, this mistake shouldn’t be made. If you have had a

technical job in the past, bring it out in the resume. Don’t remove the points thinking that you are

in business school now and companies will only hire you if you have non-tech points in your

resume. PM companies like to see tech on the resumes.

Gyan 3: Follow Gyan 2, but present the technical points in an appropriate manner. You have done

16

tech in the past. That’s good. Put that in the resume, but only in a proper format. A point like this:

Designed satellite ground-station code using PIL, Numpy, Pandas, OpenCV Python3 libraries

     Is good but not that helpful for a PM resume. Remember, the role you are applying for is NOT

a coding role, and spelling out Python libraries or frameworks will only consume precious resume

space and not convey much to the interviewer in terms of the impact of what you did. A better

point might be:

Part of successful satellite launch, developer of spacecraft and ground station software

Why is it better? Because it removes the technicalities and makes the point more readable for a

screener, who might not have a technical background. Also, it conveys some technical impact to a

screener who is looking for someone to create a technical impact during the internship. The

general advise here is that bring out the technical experience but present it in a way that shows

the magnitude of the impact on the company you were working for (either on the company

operations or on the financials). By writing your points in a way that show impact, it will convey to

the screener that what you did in the past mattered to the organization.

Gyan 4: If you have dealt with any sort of new project/product in the past, highlight that as

project/product management. Companies that come on campus will put you on a team that is

managing a product. And management requires responsibility and decisive abilities. Thus, let that

come out in your resume by showcasing appropriate past responsibilities.

In my work experience, I had managed a small product before, and I mentioned that in the

resume. During the interview, they then asked me about that specific point, because it was highly

appropriate for the role.

Gyan 5: Highly inflated numbers is not a must. As you go through the multiple iterations of

making your resume, you might receive advice like ‘ without having inflated numbers the

company will throw your resume out’ etc. That’s not true. Having inflated numbers in your resume

might help, but not having them doesn’t mean you are going to get rejected, because many

companies know that the numbers on students’ resumes are exaggerated. The biggest number I

had in my resume was in terms of thousands of dollars. Not impressive by any scale if compared

to the Crores that some people had mentioned.

In fact, inflated numbers might also turn out to be a curse if you are suddenly asked to explain

during the interview exactly how you arrived at that number. For example, Amazon, during the

interviews, did ask its potential hires how they arrived at some of the numbers mentioned in the

resume.

You are not ready to build the perfect resume. That’s great. Let’s move onto the other hygiene

PPTs of PM Companies: If you don’t know what a Pre-Placement Talk (PPT) is right now, you will

find out soon enough. Several PM companies give PPTs before the placement week. How useful

is it to attend these PPTs? In my view, moderately useful. Sometimes they talk about new

might become an interview question. Some companies like Zynga even offer Pre-placement

you can be to find out which company is giving the PPT and ask around whether the company

does anything more than introductions in its PPT.

Case Studies/Design Competitions: Taking part in these competitions can be a good way for the

company recruiters to get to know your ideas (in case they are involved in the judging process).

And if you win, you might also get a PPI.

Completing PM tasks such as wireframe design development: Often during the course of online

applications, many companies will open tasks that will require you to maybe make a mobile

application using wireframes or other techniques. Some companies like Media.net require you to

complete these tasks so that they can judge your thinking process before the interviews. Do look

out for these activities to get a place in those companies interview panels.

looking to get into PM roles. Preparing in a group has several advantages:

Does prep for other fields help in prep for a PM role?

This is a very frequent question that gets asked. To be honest, prep for Finance doesn’t help in

prepping for a PM role, because finance prep is knowledge-heavy and the knowledge that you

will be learning for a finance role is mutually exclusive to a PM role. Prep for marketing helps a

little because you might pickup some marketing frameworks while solving marketing cases. But

again, marketing requires learning different skills, and those skills might not be directly applicable

to a PM role.

On the other hand, prep for consulting does help. In fact, one of the resources mentioned above

(Case Interviews Cracked) is a book that is often used while preparing for consulting cases. And

many people used this book while preparing for PM roles as well. The reason why prep for

consulting is useful while preparing for PM is that consulting prep teaches you how to structure

your thoughts and break down problems to come up with the best solution despite a lack of

information. And that is a very useful skill to have during a PM interview. Most of the time, the

prime concern of the interviewer is to check how well you structure problems to come up with the

best solution under given constraints.

Despite consulting prep being useful while preparing for a PM role, it is just a subset of the total

prep required for a PM role, because a PM role also needs knowledge of technology and

guesstimate-solving skills. So even if you do decide to prep for consulting, be prepared to put in

extra effort to gain knowledge that is very specific to a PM role.

Resume Based
Questions

21

Finally, let’s get to the serious stuff. The first type of question we are going to look at are resume-

based questions.

What is your background? How much do you know about technology? Product Management is

about solving customers’ problems, and almost all of those problems are technical in nature. So a

prerequisite to becoming a PM intern is to know about technology, and you will have to

demonstrate that knowledge in some form during the interview or GD.

The first piece of information that a company gets about you is your resume. So it is necessary to

work at preparing a good resume and once the resume is finalized, it is necessary to prepare

answering any question that the interviewer raises on points in your resume. It is your resume, and

you have spent the effort in writing it. So the interviewer will expect you to know each and every

point in your resume. And to reinstate the point, if you have included numbers in your resume, be

ready to explain to the interviewer how you arrived at the number. The larger the number, the

more explanation from your side, it will need to convince the interviewer.

Some of the possible questions that can be asked on your resume are given below:

1)  How did you arrive at this number?

2)  So you have mentioned here that you have done project XX in the past. What was it

about? What was the impact on the company’s operations/revenue?

3)  Walk me through your resume

I can’t give you a right or perfect answer to the questions mentioned above. But I can say that in

my case, a lot of my resume points are based on startups and international experiences, and so

some of the questions I was asked were:

1)  You have worked in small-companies your whole life. Was that a deliberate decision or

something that you were forced to do?

2)  Why choose a big company now?

3)  What made you come to study in IIMB?

4)  What were you doing in country XX?

I did try to link every answer to the questions above to the role I was interviewing for. For

example, for the question, Why choose a big company now?, I remember mentioning something

22

along the following lines:

Me: Working in a small company is a very interesting experience but everything is on a small

scale. A small market is targeted and customers are few. In a big company, I will be exposed to

the same things that I did in a small company, but at a much larger scale…….

Yeah, I know, globe. As long as the globe manages to convince the interviewer, it’s alright.

We talked about some tips to remember while making a PM resume. Here is another point that

relates to the formatting of the points rather than the content. The suggested format for

structuring points in your resume is as follows:

Power-Verb (preferably bolded), followed by business impact and a number (preferably bolded

again), followed by some technical detail

Starting the point with a power verb helps to attract the reader to the point and shows action on

your part. Stating a business impact shows how your work contributed to the business of the

company, highlighting its importance in the larger scheme of things, and stating a number

concretizes the business impact. Finally, the technical detail can highlight your technical expertise

or also work as a filler to make the point fill the entire line.

Do remember to practise enough of these resume-based questions so that you can recite each

project/responsibility/experience on your resume inside-out.

Chapter 5

HR Questions

24

HR questions, including behavioural questions, are those sets of general questions that might

seem easy to answer at first glance, but can be deceptive because depending on how you

answer, they can give away facets of your character and behaviour. HR questions are open-ended

and can be answered in several ways. Thus they become good points to judge honesty, integrity,

self-knowledge, existing biases, and other character-based traits you might engender. And with a

lot of companies nowadays placing heavy premium on hiring candidates that ‘fit the company

culture’, answering these questions well becomes as much of a necessity as of answering the

other technical questions well.

Listed below are some often-heard HR questions:

Introduce yourself

Many companies that you interview with will ask this question right at the beginning. Even though

they have your resume in front, they still want to hear about you from your own mouth.

Why?

I don’t know. Maybe the way you answer the question serves as a truth filter because they can

verify whether what you say matches what’s written in your resume.

Does that mean I can answer the question in any way I like?

You can, but it’s better to prepare a rough answer that makes a positive impact on the

interviewer.

Whenever I was asked this question, I treated it as an opportunity to summarize my experience

and signal that I was fit for the role being offered, and all within 2 minutes.

I kept the following things in mind:
  I have to talk about something unique I have done in the field the role was in
  I have to mention my experience/achievements in the field
  I have to give them the image that I am the right person for the job

When the interviewer at Microsoft, for the role of PM, asked me the question, I did a brief run-

through of my job experiences, technical degree, and also talked about the time I had designed

small-scale products for one of the companies I used to work for. I changed the reply when an

Analytics company asked me the question. At that time, I talked about the large amounts of data I

had handled in the past.

Now, specific for a PM role, covering these points should help create an impact on the

25

interviewer:

1)  Technical projects or work done in the past => The idea behind mentioning your projects

and experience is to highlight your familiarity with technology and assure the interviewer

that you won’t be overwhelmed when put in charge of a technical product.

2)  A leadership role, or a product/project management role in the past => By your

mentioning that you have already worked as a team leader or as a product manager

highlights your fit for the role.

Guesstimates

29

Moving onto to technical questions, the first category of questions we will touch will be called

guesstimates. Guesstimates are questions which require a number as an answer, but reaching that

number from the question will require you to take knowledgeable guesses and use logical

thinking. The question will often seem vague and unanswerable. Examples include “Calculate the

weight of Mount Everest” or “How many police stations there are in India”.

The idea behind these questions is not to come up with the correct number, but to break down

the problem and solve it step-by-step so that you can come up to a reasonable guess of the

actual answer. For example, if you are asked to calculate the weight of Mount Everest, you will

have to break down the problem into several steps, and at each step along the way, make

reasonable assumptions. By combining these reasonable assumptions, you can then come up to a

final figure, which most likely won’t be true, but will be an estimate of the actual value.

Why are these questions important? Because many times during your internship/jobs, you might

be expected to take a decision, but full information is not available for you to make the correct

decision.

Should I release this product now?

What questions should I include in customer surveys?

Should the marketing campaign touch upon these themes?

These are some of the questions you might need to answer but don’t have proper information

about. That’s when having the ability to reasonably guess a correct answer becomes handy.

For you to get a better idea of what to do when a Guesstimate question does come up, let’s solve an

example question and come up to a concrete answer:

Question: What is the weight of Mount Everest?

Step 1: Breakdown the question and resolve any follow-up questions you encounter

Now you might be thinking, What a stupid question? Who would want to know that? And more

importantly, how would I go about solving such a question?

The first step to solving this problem is to take the question, break it down word-by-word, and focus

only on the words that are important. What is the weight of Mount Everest.

The two bolded words are important. They mean that you need to come up with a kilogram estimate

for the weight of the famous mountain.

Step 2: Define the scope and assumptions of your problem

30

Great, so you have to calculate the weight of the mountain. What next?

In my experience, there are only two methods of finding out the weight of something: Either you

measure the weight using a weighing machine, or you use the ancient, yet trustworthy, Physics

formula:

Density = Mass/Volume

You can’t weight Mount Everest on a weighing machine, so that leaves us with the second option of

finding the weight.

So you have determined that you need to find out the volume and density of the mountain. Since

volume can only be found for regular shapes, you can assume that the mountain is a regular cone,

and the volume of a cone is 1/3*Pi*Radius2*Height.

So we have assumed the mountain to be a cone. What is the cone’s angle? We can make the
assumption here that the angle is 45o, based on the reasoning that the mountain has a very wide base
(so that it can support such a large structure) with an angle of maybe 30 o, and tourist photos show it

has a steep top of maybe 60 o. Averaging the two numbers out will give you a value of 45 o.

What about the density? That will depend on the material that the mountain is made up off.

Here we will make an assumption that the mountain is made up of uniform rock with a single density.

We know that there are other things such as trees and snow on the surface, but we are going to

ignore those items, and calculate the weight of only the bare mountain.

Step 3: Form the equation

We have made the following assumptions in our quest to solve the problem:

1)  The mountain is a regular cone with a volume calculated using the equation

1/3*Pi*Radius2*Height
The cone’s angle is 45o

2)  The mountain is made of uniform rock throughout

Remember to openly state these assumptions and inform the interviewer that you are making these

reasonable guesses based on the above-mentioned criteria

We are going to use the equation Mass = Density x Volume

Mass = Density x 1/3*Pi*Radius2*Height

31

Pi * 1/3 is almost equal to 1

Height is almost 9000m
Radius is also almost 9000m (Using the 45 o value for angle means the base of the triangle is going to

be equal to the height.

Thus, R2H = 9000*9000*9000 = 7.3 x 1011

Volume of the mountain: 7.3 x 1011 m3

Density of normal rock = 3000 kg/m3

Thus, the mass = 7.3 x 1011 x 3000 = 2.2 x 1015 KG

Mount Everest weighs 2.2 x 1015 KG

Great. You have successfully calculated the weight of Mount Everest. A few points to note here:

1)  The numbers taken might seem very crude, but remember that at that moment, you won’t

have access to the internet, and so all the numbers and equations you use have to be ones

that you remember of the top of your mind

2)  Explicitly state to the interviewer all the assumptions you are making throughout the

calculation process. Solving guesstimates is not a test of accuracy.

3)  What matters here is your process of solving the question, more than the actual answer itself.

So even if the answer isn’t completely correct, explicitly demonstrate your thinking process to

the interviewer.

For this chapter, I am going to differentiate between new product design and product

New Product Design

So you are tasked with designing a new product. How do you go about it? What items should you

consider while building the product? How will you measure success? The framework below should

help you to understand what is required and come up with a product.

Thoroughly understand the problem: The problem provided to you will generally be in the form

of a question. Some questions can be very vague, such as ‘Design a clock’, while others can be

very specific, such as ‘Design a clock for blind people’. You might think that the more vague the

question, the easier it is to solve. In reality, it is the opposite. The less vague the question, the

easier it is to solve because understanding vague questions takes more effort from your side.

If you vaguely are asked to ‘Design a clock’, you will have to ask the following questions just to

understand what the problem means:

There are many types of clocks. Which clock is the interviewer referring to? Alarm clock?

Grandfather clock? Souvenir clock?

Who is going to use the clock?

You will have to explicitly ask the interviewer questions to get the same amount of information

that is provided in a specific problem statement like ‘Design an alarm clock for blind people’

Do get as much information here as you can because only after thoroughly understanding what

needs to be solved will you be able to design a correct solution.

35

Identify Users & Customers: Every product is designed to be sold. When the problem statement

given to you specifies a product to be designed, there is already a target user for the product in

the mind of the interviewer. Two scenarios can play out here. Either the interviewer provides the

user segment to you, or the interviewer asks you to determine the right user segment.

For very specific questions, such as ‘Design an alarm clock for blind people’, the user is already

defined as blind people. For vague questions, ask the interviewer about this information, and if

the interviewer declines to give it to you, you make an assumption, state it out loud, and stick to

that assumption throughout the product designing process. You can say something along the

lines of:

I’m going to design a souvenir clock for tourists to take back to their home countries. Incoming

tourists are my target market.

Sometimes, the users of a product and the customers can be different people. The difference

between users and customers is that users use the product, while customers buy the product. And

this difference is usually present in products that are being designed for kids to use but for

parents/teachers to buy. Do find out from the interviewer if that is the case for the product you

are designing.

revenue per period, user registrations per period etc.

I want to mention a closing pointer here about defining absolute versus relative metrics. Absolute

metrics measure the metric in a certain time period, for example downloads per week. Relative

metrics measure the same metric over time. For example whether downloads per week have been

increasing or decreasing over the past year. Absolute metrics are useful comparison points across

competing products. Which application has more downloads per week? Ola or Uber? And relative

metrics are useful comparison points for the same product over time. Is Uber seeing increasing

downloads per week over the past year or is it seeing a decreasing trend? Those data points can

point to positive or negative trends about product adoption.

Big-O Notation

The Big-O notation is used to express algorithm complexity in a simplified notation. Using an

algorithm’s Big-O value, you can determine how the algorithm will scale as the number of inputs

to the algorithm increases. The lower the Big-O value of an algorithm, the more scalable it is and

lesser resources it will need.

The most common Big-O notations are:

1)  O(1) => The time taken to run through the algorithm is independent of the number of

items in the array. Also called constant-time algorithm.

2)  O(log n) => Time taken is proportional to the log of the number of items in the array.
3)  O(n) => Time taken is proportional to number of items
4)  O(n log n) => Time taken is proportional to number of items x log of number of items
5)  O(n2) => Time taken is proportional to number of items squared.

Data Structures

As the name implies, data structures are algorithmic structures for storing and handling data. The

most common ones are:

1)  Array

The most popular and common data structure, arrays are single-dimensional lists of elements.

Elements (alphanumeric/memory addresses etc.) can be addressed by calling the ‘index’ of the

array. Array indices start from 0.

2)  Hashtables

A structure which stores data in the form of key-value pairs. A ‘key’ is often a unique value

representing something about an object, while a value represents characteristics of the object. For

example, in a library, books are given unique numbers. These numbers can become ‘keys’ in the

hashtable. Furthermore, each book has several characteristics such as Name, Author, Publishing

House etc. which can become ‘values’ associated with that ‘key’. To look up information about the

book, search for the serial number of the book in the hashtable and read the associated values

with the key.

3)  Linked-List

A structure with data-nodes linked to each other using pointers. Each node has a data field and a

pointer field that points to the address of the next node. Linked-lists seem to be more

complicated than arrays even though both of them serve the same purpose, but their advantage

is that inserting/deleting nodes in a linked-list is easier as compared to doing that in other data

structures. Also, linked-lists are dynamic data-structures, meaning that nodes can be

added/removed even after the structure is declared, and unlike static structures like arrays that

have to be pre-defined with a fixed size.

4)  Stacks

54

A stack is like an array, but with limited operations that can be performed on it. Stacks are LIFO

(Last In First Out) structures, meaning that the last element added to the stack will be the first

element popped out. Only two operations can be carried out on a stack: push, pop. Pushing

involves pushing elements onto the top of the stack, and popping involves taking out elements

from the top of the stack.

5)  Queues

Queues are similar to stacks, but they operate on a FIFO (First In First Out) basis. Similar to stacks,

only two operations can be performed on queues: enqueueing, dequeueing. Enqueueing means

inserting elements on the back of the queue. Dequeueing means removing elements from the

front of the queue.

6)  Trees

A tree is a structure that is a collection of nodes, but arranged in the form of a tree, with each

node spawning a set of children nodes, and those children nodes spawning their own set of

children nodes. The diagram below shows an example of a tree with multiple hierarchies of

Searching Algorithms

Search algorithms are used to search for data in any data structure. Out of all the existent

searching algorithms, the most prominent ones are:

1)  Binary Search

55

This algorithm is used to search for a specific term in a sorted data structure, usually an array. The

algorithm starts by taking the query to be searched, and comparing it to the middle value of the

array. If the query is larger than the middle value, it means that the value has to be in the right half

of the array. If the query is smaller than the middle value, it means that the value has to be in the

left half of the array. Once the comparison between the desired value and the middle value is

made, the other half of the array is discarded. These steps continue until the desired value is

found. If it is not found, a null can be returned.

Thus in each run, half of the array is discarded, bringing the complexity of the array to a worst-

case scenario of O(log n). The photo below represents the simplified operation of the algorithm.

Source: https://www.topcoder.com/wp-content/uploads/2017/07/binary-search.png

2)  Linear Search

Another search algorithm that simply involves searching for the desired element through an array

by going through each element of the array one-by-one until the element is found. Thus, the

worst-case time complexity of a linear search algorithm is O(n).

3)  Basics of Breadth-First Search and Depth-First Search

These algorithms might not be necessary to understand, but I guess it’s still good to know what

these terms mean. Breadth-First Search (BFS) and Depth-First Search (DFS) are more complicated

search algorithms done on graph data-structures. In a BFS implementation, the graph is searched

56

horizontally, covering the entire breadth of the graph. If the desired term is not found, the

algorithm begins searching the tree one level below what it was searching in previously.

In a DFS implementation, the graph is searched vertically, by searching a node, and then its child

node, and then that child nodes child node, until the search algorithm reaches the bottom of the

graph. If the desired term is not found, the search goes back to the top and starts again adjacent

to the node on which it began search in the previous iteration. The diagrams below hopefully give

you a better idea of how BFS and DFS work.

Sorting Algorithms

Out of the several sorting algorithms, the most well-known ones are:

1)  Bubble Sort

The bubble sort algorithm begins at the start of an array, and compares the value in that cell to

the cell in front. If the value is smaller, it will swap the two cells. After each swap, the algorithm will

move forward by one cell. One iteration of the algorithm will be over when the last element in the

array is reached. If the array is not sorted then, another iteration will begin from the first value

again. This process goes on until the entire array is sorted either in an ascending or descending

order. Due to the algorithm having to move through the array multiple times, the worst-case time

complexity of the swap is O(n2). The image below will help explain the algorithm more clearly:

2)  Selection Sort

In a selection sort algorithm, the array is traversed to find the smallest element. Once that

element is located, it is switched with the first element of the array. The algorithm then goes

through the array again to find the second smallest element in the array and switches that value

with the second element in the array. This process repeats until the entire array is sorted. Because

the algorithm has to go through an array multiple times, the worst-case time complexity of this
algorithm is also O(n2).
