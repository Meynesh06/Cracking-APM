# 06 AI Product Strategy and Economics

AI features change the economics of a product. Every request has a real marginal cost (model inference), so margins, pricing and build decisions must be reasoned about per task, not only per user. This chapter covers how to source models, how to measure and price AI cost per unit of value, and how to position an AI product so it is defensible.

## Subsections
- [[6.1 Build vs buy vs API access]] - choosing between training your own model, hosting an open model, or calling a vendor API.
- [[6.2 Unit economics - cost per task, inference cost, gross margin]] - turning token and inference cost into per-task and per-user margin.
- [[6.3 Pricing models - per seat, usage-based, outcome-based, bundled]] - how to charge for AI when cost scales with usage.
- [[6.4 Defensibility - proprietary data, distribution, workflow integration, switching costs]] - why a wrapper around a model is not a moat by itself.
- [[6.5 Copilot vs automation vs autonomous agent]] - the autonomy spectrum and how it drives cost, risk and pricing.

## Key takeaways
- Model cost is a variable cost of goods sold; price and design against it from day one.
- API access is the fastest route to market; it becomes a margin problem at scale.
- Usage-based or outcome-based pricing protects margin when cost tracks usage.
- A moat comes from data, distribution, workflow depth or switching costs, not from the model alone.
- Higher autonomy raises both value and risk; scope it to what you can evaluate and afford.

# 6.1 Build vs buy vs API access
> Start with a vendor API to learn what users want, then move down the stack only when cost, control or data needs justify it.

## Concept
There are three broad sourcing options for model capability:

| Option | What you do | Pros | Cons |
|---|---|---|---|
| Build (train your own) | Pretrain or heavily train a model | Full control, possible moat | Very expensive, needs scarce talent and data |
| Host an open-weight model | Fine-tune and run an open-weight model (e.g. Llama, Mistral, Qwen, DeepSeek families) on your infra or a cloud | Lower marginal cost at steady high volume, data stays in your VPC, no surprise deprecations | You own serving, scaling, evals and upgrades; usually behind the frontier on hardest tasks |
| API access | Call a hosted model (OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, etc.), directly or via a cloud (Azure, AWS Bedrock, Google Vertex) | Fastest, best-in-class quality, no infra, prices tend to fall over time | Vendor model changes and deprecations, data-handling terms, rate limits, less control |

Decision factors for a PM:
- **Differentiation:** Is the model itself the product, or is it a component? Most products are the latter.
- **Data sensitivity:** Regulated or confidential data may force self-hosting or a private deployment.
- **Volume and unit cost:** API cost is linear in usage; self-hosting has a large fixed cost (GPUs, engineers) and only becomes cheaper at high, steady utilisation. Because API prices for a given capability have fallen fast, the break-even point keeps moving; re-check it yearly.
- **Quality bar:** If a frontier API meets the eval bar, the premium for building is rarely justified.
- **Team capability:** Serving and evaluating models is an ongoing platform cost, not a one-off project.

The real-world default in 2025-26 is a mix: an API for hard, low-volume tasks and a small or open model for narrow, high-volume ones, behind a thin routing layer.

A common path: prototype on an API, measure quality and usage with evals, then consider routing high-volume, narrow tasks to a smaller or fine-tuned model. Keep the abstraction layer thin so you can switch vendors.

## Real-world examples
- **GitHub Copilot** - launched on OpenAI models; since late 2024 it has let users choose models from several vendors (including Anthropic and Google). GitHub owns the IDE integration and product experience, so the model is a swappable component (buy-and-integrate).
- **Duolingo Max** - launched in 2023 on GPT-4 for features such as "Explain my answer" and roleplay, while Duolingo focused on pedagogy and UX.
- **Sarvam AI (India)** - builds its own models for Indian languages and voice, and in April 2025 was selected under the government's IndiaAI Mission to build a sovereign foundation model: the build path, justified by language coverage and sovereignty rather than by cost.
- **Freshworks Freddy AI (India-founded SaaS)** - generative features such as reply suggestions and ticket summaries built on third-party models combined with Freshworks' own customer data: "buy the model, own the workflow".
- **BloombergGPT (2023)** - Bloomberg trained its own 50-billion-parameter finance model. Later evaluations showed general frontier models matching or beating it on many finance tasks, a cautionary tale for training from scratch.

## Practice questions
1. A healthtech startup wants AI summaries of patient notes. Build, host, or API? What would change your answer?
   - Outline: data residency and compliance push toward private hosting or an API covered by a health-data agreement (in the US, a HIPAA Business Associate Agreement; in India, DPDP-compliant processor terms); start with the API for quality, measure, then decide on hosting.
2. Your API cost doubled after a vendor price change. What do you do in the next two weeks?
   - Outline: check per-task cost, route simple requests to a cheaper model, cache, and set a fallback to an open model.
3. When would you tell leadership not to train a foundation model?
   - Outline: almost always, unless the model itself is the product, you have unique data at scale, a gap that APIs and open models cannot fill (language, sovereignty, on-device), and the budget to keep retraining as the frontier moves. Otherwise fine-tune or prompt an existing model and invest in workflow and data.
4. How do you keep vendor lock-in low without over-engineering?
   - Outline: one internal interface for model calls, prompts and evals stored outside vendor tools, the eval suite as the switching test, and a tested fallback model. Avoid building an abstraction over every vendor feature.

# 6.2 Unit economics - cost per task, inference cost, gross margin
> In AI products, the unit that matters is the cost of one completed task, and gross margin is set by how many tasks a paying user triggers.

## Concept
Traditional SaaS has near-zero marginal cost per user. AI features add inference cost on every call, so the unit economics must be built bottom-up:

**Cost per task** = calls per task x [(input tokens x input price) + (output tokens x output price)] + retrieval/tool costs + infra overhead (+ retries and fallbacks)

Calls per task matters: an agent or a reasoning model may make many calls or produce thousands of hidden reasoning tokens for one user request, so a feature that looks cheap per call can be expensive per task. Measure **cost per successful task** (total cost ÷ tasks that actually succeeded), because failed attempts still cost money.

Worked example (illustrative prices): a support answer uses 3,000 input tokens and 400 output tokens. At $1 per million input and $4 per million output, that is $0.003 + $0.0016 ≈ $0.005 per call. With 1.3 calls per task (retries, a safety check) and an 80% success rate, cost per successful task ≈ $0.005 x 1.3 ÷ 0.8 ≈ $0.008.

**Cost per paying user** = tasks per user per month x cost per task

**Gross margin** = (revenue per user - cost of serving that user) / revenue per user, where cost of serving (COGS) includes inference, hosting, and third-party APIs.

Classic SaaS gross margins are often 75-80%+; AI-heavy products have commonly reported lower margins because inference is a real COGS line.

Key levers:
- **Model choice:** a smaller model can be 10x cheaper per token or more, and is often good enough for a narrow task.
- **Prompt length and context:** long retrieved context inflates input tokens; trim it.
- **Output length:** output tokens are usually priced higher; cap or structure responses.
- **Caching:** repeated prompts or semantic caches cut cost.
- **Routing:** send easy requests to cheap models, hard ones to expensive models.
- **Retries:** failed or re-generated calls are hidden cost; measure them.
- **Usage distribution:** averages hide heavy users. A small group of power users can make a plan unprofitable, so model the tail.

A PM should be able to write the sensitivity table: how margin changes if tokens per task go up 2x, if price per token falls 50%, or if the feature's adoption doubles. The question to answer is whether the feature stays profitable at the 95th-percentile user, not the average one.

## Real-world examples
- **GitHub Copilot** - The Wall Street Journal reported in October 2023 that GitHub was losing money on many users of its $10-a-month plan, with heavy users costing far more than they paid (the report cited unnamed sources). In 2025 GitHub added monthly allowances of "premium requests" for advanced models, with paid overage: a usage-metered layer on top of a per-seat plan.
- **Intercom Fin** - priced per resolution (launched at $0.99 per resolution in 2023), so Intercom's cost per resolution, including failed attempts that do not resolve, has to stay well below that price.
- **Indic-language products** - with older tokenisers, the same sentence in Hindi or Tamil could take several times more tokens than in English, so cost per task differed by language. Tokeniser improvements (for example, OpenAI's GPT-4o tokeniser in 2024) narrowed the gap; measure tokens per task for each language before setting prices.

## Practice questions
1. Your AI summarizer costs Rs 4 per summary and users generate 30 per month on a Rs 499 plan. Is the feature profitable? What would you change?
   - Outline: 30 x Rs 4 = Rs 120, about 24% of plan price before other costs; check the tail users, route short documents to a cheaper model, cap length.
2. How would you forecast AI COGS for a launch with no usage data?
   - Outline: estimate tokens per task from prototype logs, a range of tasks per user, then a best/base/worst case; plan to revisit after two weeks.
3. Why might a feature with 70% gross margin on average still be a problem?
   - Outline: the average hides heavy users who may be margin-negative; usage grows over time; agent features or longer contexts can raise tokens per task; price competition may cut revenue; and 70% is below typical SaaS margins, which affects valuation.
4. What metric would you put on a dashboard to catch cost regressions early?
   - Outline: cost per successful task by feature and model version, tokens per task (input and output), calls per task, and the share of users whose cost exceeds their revenue, with alerts on week-over-week jumps.

# 6.3 Pricing models - per seat, usage-based, outcome-based, bundled
> Pick the pricing metric that moves with the value the customer gets and with your cost, so revenue and margin grow together.

## Concept
The Sigma casebook framework lists the common non-hardware pricing options as ad-based, periodic subscription, and freemium-style tiers. AI products add two more metrics that are tied to cost:

| Model | Charge based on | Good when | Risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Per seat | Users per month | Value scales with headcount; predictable revenue | Heavy users cost more than seat price; light users underpay |
| Usage-based | Tokens, requests, documents, minutes | Cost tracks usage closely | Hard for buyers to budget; churn when bills spike |
| Outcome-based | Successful task or resolution | Outcome is measurable and agreed | Needs trustworthy attribution; revenue volatile |
| Credits | Prepaid credits spent on different AI actions | Many features with different costs | Opaque to buyers; credit inflation |
| Bundled | Included in a higher plan or suite | Drives upgrades; raises perceived value | Hides AI cost; hard to price-discriminate |
| Hybrid | Seat fee + usage credits | Most enterprise AI deals | More complex to explain |

Guidelines:
- Price on the metric closest to value and cost (e.g., resolution, document, or seat).
- Keep a free or low-cost tier only if its cost per user is bounded (rate limits, smaller model).
- Include a usage allowance inside the seat price to avoid bill shock; charge overage above it.
- Check the Sigma pricing step: a product that is bundled may still need a stated price anchor for the interview answer.

## Real-world examples
- **GitHub Copilot** - a free tier (from December 2024), individual and business per-seat plans, and monthly "premium request" allowances with paid overage for advanced models (per-seat plus usage).
- **Microsoft 365 Copilot** - launched for enterprises in 2023 as a $30 per user per month add-on (seat-based add-on). In January 2025 Microsoft folded Copilot into consumer Microsoft 365 Personal and Family plans and raised their prices, the bundling move. Google did the same with Gemini in Workspace business plans the same month.
- **Intercom Fin** - priced per resolution (launched at $0.99 per resolution), the outcome-based model.
- **Salesforce Agentforce** - launched in 2024 at $2 per conversation, then added credit-based and per-user options in 2025, showing how vendors iterate on AI pricing metrics as buyers ask for predictability.
- **Spotify (from the July 2022 teardown)** - Free (ad-supported) and Premium (ad-free, offline) tiers, a freemium plus subscription structure that AI features could be bundled into.
- **Sportskeeda (Feb 2023 teardown)** - primarily advertising-funded, with premium and subscription sections added to diversify revenue; a case of ad revenue being a weak base for a new feature.

## Practice questions
1. You are launching an AI meeting-notes feature in a collaboration tool. Seat, usage, or bundle? Justify.
   - Outline: bundle into the paid tier to drive upgrades, with a usage cap for transcription minutes; add a paid add-on for heavy use.
2. A customer says your per-resolution price is unpredictable. How do you respond?
   - Outline: offer a committed monthly bundle with resolution credits and an overage rate; show the cost-per-resolution trend.
3. When is free the right price for an AI feature?
   - Outline: when cost per user is small and bounded (small model, caps), when the feature drives acquisition, retention, or upgrades worth more than its cost, or when competitors give it away. Cap usage and keep the expensive capability for paid tiers.
4. How do you decide between an add-on price and folding AI into the base plan?
   - Outline: add-on when value is concentrated in some users, cost per user is high, and you need to prove willingness to pay; fold in when adoption is the goal, value is broad, and cost per user is low enough to absorb, usually with a base-price increase.

# 6.4 Defensibility - proprietary data, distribution, workflow integration, switching costs
> If every competitor can call the same model, the moat has to come from data, distribution, the workflow you sit in, or the cost of leaving.

## Concept
Model quality is commoditizing: capability that was frontier a year ago becomes cheap and widely available. A wrapper around a model has little defensibility, so the PM must answer "why won't a well-funded competitor, or the model vendor itself, copy this in a quarter?"

Sources of defensibility for AI products:
- **Proprietary data:** unique, consented, labelled data or domain corpora that improve the product and are hard to obtain. Data alone is not a moat unless it is used to improve outcomes (see 4.2 on data flywheels).
- **Distribution:** an existing user base, channel or platform that the AI feature plugs into (for example, a messaging app or an e-commerce checkout).
- **Workflow integration:** the AI sits inside a system of record, with permissions, approvals and integrations. Removing it breaks daily work.
- **Switching costs:** data gravity, retrained teams, custom configurations, integrations and contracts.
- **Trust and brand:** in regulated or high-stakes domains, a proven safety record is slow to replicate.
- **Cost position:** a lower cost per task that lets you price below rivals and still earn margin.

Test: remove the model vendor from the equation. What is left that a customer would miss? If the answer is only the prompt, the moat is weak.

## Real-world examples
- **Bloomberg** - its moat is proprietary financial data plus the Terminal workflow. It trained BloombergGPT (2023), but general frontier models soon matched it on many finance tasks, which suggests the durable moat is the data and workflow, not the model.
- **Microsoft 365 Copilot** - draws on the Microsoft Graph of user documents and emails and sits inside existing Office workflows, an example of distribution plus workflow integration.
- **Blinkit (Feb 2023 teardown)** - the teardown's ideas (ingredient kits, subscriptions for daily essentials) lean on an existing dense delivery network and user habit, which is distribution-based defensibility.
- **Epic Systems (US healthcare)** - deep EHR integration creates switching costs that a new AI tool must work inside rather than replace; Epic has added generative AI features (such as drafting replies to patient messages, built with Microsoft) inside its own workflow.
- **Cursor vs GitHub Copilot** - the AI code editor Cursor grew quickly despite Copilot's distribution advantage inside VS Code and GitHub, a reminder that distribution slows rivals but does not stop a clearly better workflow.

## Practice questions
1. A startup offers an AI legal-drafting tool built on a frontier API. Where is its moat, and how would you test it?
   - Outline: check whether it owns firm-specific templates and clause data, integrations with document systems, and retention after a model upgrade; run a churn-risk test with a vendor-neutral baseline.
2. Why might a model vendor be a competitor to your product?
   - Outline: vendors see aggregate demand through their APIs and move up the stack into popular use cases (for example, assistants adding coding, search, and document features). If your product is a thin wrapper on their model, a single launch can match it.
3. Which defensibility lever matters most for an Indian consumer app with a large user base?
   - Outline: distribution and habit first (the user base and daily use), then proprietary behavioural data in local languages that improves the feature, and trust (payments, KYC) in regulated categories.
4. How would you measure switching costs in a product?
   - Outline: integrations per account, data volume stored, number of customised workflows, time to migrate, and churn after competitor launches or price increases.

# 6.5 Copilot vs automation vs autonomous agent
> Autonomy is a dial: the more the AI acts without a human, the more value it can create and the more risk, cost and evaluation burden you take on.

## Concept
Three product postures sit on one spectrum:

| Posture | Who decides | Example behaviour | Typical risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Copilot (assistive) | Human, AI suggests | Drafts text, code completion, suggested replies | Low; the human catches errors |
| Automation (rules + AI) | AI executes a bounded step | Auto-tags tickets, routes a claim | Medium; errors repeat at scale |
| Autonomous agent | AI plans and executes multi-step tasks | Books, files, or resolves end to end using tools | High; wrong actions have real consequences |

Why it matters for strategy and economics:
- **Value:** autonomy removes more human effort, so it can justify higher prices (outcome-based pricing fits here).
- **Cost:** agents call models many times per task (plan, act, check), so cost per task rises; see 6.2.
- **Evaluation:** a copilot is judged on acceptance; an agent needs end-to-end task success, step-level traces and safety evals (see 8.1 and 8.4).
- **Trust:** moving down the spectrum requires earned confidence, typically by starting as a copilot, logging accept/edit rates, then widening scope.
- **Reversibility:** actions that are reversible (drafts) can be autonomous sooner than irreversible ones (payments, emails sent to customers).

Analogy that works in interviews: self-driving cars' levels of autonomy (driver assistance to full self-driving). Waymo runs fully driverless robotaxis in limited areas after years of testing and remote support; most cars ship only driver assistance. AI products climb the same ladder, area by area.

A PM decision rule: start at the lowest autonomy that delivers the value, earn the right to go higher with measured acceptance and error data, and put approval gates on any irreversible step.

## Real-world examples
- **GitHub Copilot** - the canonical copilot: suggestions appear in the editor and the developer accepts or rejects them.
- **Intercom Fin** - a support agent that resolves conversations end to end for many queries, with handoff to humans for the rest (agent-like, scoped by intent).
- **Cognition's Devin** - announced in March 2024 as an autonomous software engineering agent; independent tests reported low end-to-end success on real tasks, which shows how hard the high-autonomy end is to evaluate.
- **Ticket auto-triage in help desks (Zendesk, Freshdesk)** - AI classifies and routes tickets: bounded automation, where an error repeats at scale but is easy to reverse.
- **Duolingo Max** - "Explain my answer" is a copilot-style feature that keeps the learner in control.

## Practice questions
1. You are asked to add an AI agent that can issue refunds in a food delivery app. Copilot, automation, or agent? What guardrails?
   - Outline: start with a copilot that drafts the refund for agent approval; automate small refunds under a threshold with rollback and audit; require approval above a limit.
2. How does moving from copilot to agent change your metrics?
   - Outline: from acceptance and edit rate to task success, escalation rate, and cost per completed task.
3. Why can a higher-autonomy product have worse unit economics?
   - Outline: more model calls per task, retries and verification steps, monitoring and human review of failures, and the cost of errors (refunds, support), all of which can outweigh the higher price.
4. What would make you say no to an autonomous feature?
   - Outline: irreversible or high-harm actions, no reliable way to evaluate end-to-end success, regulation requiring human decision-making (credit, hiring), or error costs that exceed the labour saved.
